The clock on Bell Street

When the town of Marlow Vale voted to restore its 1892 clock tower, the argument was not about the cost. It was about the mechanism. The original clockwork, a lattice of brass gears and hanging weights, still ran, though it lost four minutes a week and needed a keeper to wind it every three days. A modern electric movement would keep near-perfect time for decades with almost no attention.

The restoration committee recommended the electric movement, and its case was practical. The grant paying for the stonework required the clock to be in reliable public service, and a mechanism that drifted, and that depended on a retired volunteer now seventy-eight, was hard to call reliable. Spare parts were no longer manufactured, so every repair meant paying a machinist to cut a gear by hand.

Opponents did not dispute the arithmetic. They argued instead about what the tower was for. A clock that had to be wound, they said, was not a defective appliance but a continuing relationship between the town and its own past. The weekly winding was a shared habit that made the clock the town's own in a way a sealed electric box never could be. Several noted that the keeper, Mr. Alvarez, trained two apprentices each year, so the skill itself was not dying with him.

The committee answered with a compromise: install the electric movement, but keep the old clockwork in the tower's glass entrance hall, wound by hand on the first Sunday of each month as a demonstration. Critics called this a museum's answer to a living question. Once the mechanism stopped keeping the town's time, they said, it would be an exhibit rather than a clock.

What the dispute shows is that keeping time carries two meanings. A town can keep accurate time, and it can keep faith with the habits that gave the clock its meaning. The vote next month will decide which kind of keeping matters more to Marlow Vale.
