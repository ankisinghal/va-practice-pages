Where the ruler begins

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original fictional passage written for practice, so full text is included.

An imagined laboratory measures the height of liquid in a sealed tube. Its first ruler begins at the bottom of the tube. A second ruler is fixed beside it with its zero ten centimetres higher. Both use centimetres of the same size. When the liquid reaches twenty on the first ruler, it reaches ten on the second. Nothing about the liquid changes when a technician looks from one ruler to the other.

Now the liquid rises by ten centimetres. The readings become thirty and twenty. On either ruler, subtracting the earlier reading from the later one gives ten. Yet the second reading has doubled, while the first has increased by half. If doubling a reading meant doubling some physical property of the liquid, the very same rise would have acquired two incompatible descriptions merely because the zero marks occupy different places.

The problem is not a defective ruler. Each reports the liquid's position relative to its own starting mark. A zero can be a useful reference without representing the absence of what is measured. On the raised ruler, zero means that the liquid has reached a particular height, not that the tube contains no liquid. Moving the reference changes the numbers but does not change the tube.

This does not make numerical comparison pointless. The two rulers agree about a ten-centimetre rise because moving the zero adds the same adjustment to both readings. Subtraction removes that adjustment. A ratio of the raw readings does not remove it. To claim that one quantity is twice another, one must therefore ask whether the scale's zero has the meaning that the claim requires, rather than trust the familiar shape of the calculation.

The laboratory's notebook keeps both sets of readings, together with the location of each zero. Beside them, the technician draws one line showing the actual change in liquid level. There are two numerical accounts, but not two rises. A measurement becomes more informative when its reference is stated, not when the reference disappears behind an impressive-looking ratio.
