What the copy cannot inherit

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original fictional passage written for practice, so full text is included.

In an imagined town, a carpenter named Saira makes an exact working copy of a wooden compass case used by a navigator. The copy opens as smoothly, protects the instrument as well and reproduces every scratch visible on the original. Visitors who handle the two cases cannot distinguish them. A collector concludes that anyone who still prefers the original must be paying for a superstition.

That conclusion assumes that the object matters only through qualities available to the hand and eye. Yet the collector himself keeps a plain envelope in which his mother sent him his first wages. He would not exchange it for a chemically identical envelope carrying a perfect copy of her writing. What he values includes a relation to a particular event. A substitute can reproduce the envelope's appearance without having taken part in that event.

The compass case can be valued similarly. Its connection with a voyage is not another scratch that Saira forgot to copy. No improvement in her workmanship could give the new case a past in which it never participated. This does not prove the old case more beautiful, more useful or more expensive. It explains how a preference can be intelligible without resting on a difference in those qualities.

There is nevertheless a danger in treating every claim of connection as self-validating. Suppose the old case was made decades after the voyage and acquired its story through a dealer's mistake. Its appearance would remain unchanged, but the historical reason for preferring it would fail. Documents and a reliable chain of ownership matter because they support the claimed relation, not because they add a hidden physical excellence to the wood.

Saira's achievement therefore needs no apology. For a sailor seeking protection for an instrument, her case may be entirely adequate; for someone seeking an object that travelled on that voyage, it cannot be a substitute. Before arguing about which case is better, ask which relationship or function the buyer is trying to acquire. An exact copy need not be a fraud, and an authentic object need not be a superior tool.
