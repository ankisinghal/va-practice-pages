The ledger in the corner shop

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

When we think of history, we often picture kings, battles and treaties. Yet some of the most useful evidence comes from far less dramatic sources. A shopkeeper's ledger, for instance, records what people bought, how much they paid and who owed money. The shopkeeper never expected anyone to read it centuries later.

Such records can be surprisingly revealing. If the same cloth appears in many entries, historians can guess what people wore. If prices rise suddenly, something may have gone wrong with harvests or trade. Entries marked as unpaid hint at how much families depended on credit and on the patience of neighbours.

Of course, ledgers have limits. They show who bought and sold, but not why. They usually leave out those who could not afford the shop at all. A historian who relied on them alone might imagine a society more comfortable than it really was.

For this reason, careful historians compare sources. A ledger may be set beside letters, court papers or songs, and each helps to correct the blind spots of the others. The picture that emerges is never complete, but it is richer than any single document could offer.
