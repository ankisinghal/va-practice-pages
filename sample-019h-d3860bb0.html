Two ways to the bus stop

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

When the town of Marlow retired its printed bus timetable last spring, the council expected few complaints. The paper sheet had been reprinted every autumn for thirty years, and its columns of tiny numbers were hard to read. The new phone app promised live arrival times, route maps and alerts when a bus ran late. Within a month the council could point to a clear gain: far more people were checking times before they left home.

Yet the picture was not simple. Mrs Oyelaran, who has ridden the number 9 for decades, said she now stood at the stop for ten minutes before she dared trust a screen that showed her bus as two minutes away and then lost it. Drivers reported that passengers without phones kept asking them for times, which slowed boarding and left the drivers uneasy about being blamed for delays they could not see. The council's reply was practical, if somewhat curt: one printed sheet would stay at the library for anyone who asked.

A local columnist argued that the debate had been framed badly. The question, she wrote, was not whether apps are better than paper but who is expected to adapt, and at what cost. A service that is easy for most people can quietly become a barrier for a few, especially when no one is assigned to notice. She suggested that the council record complaints for a year before deciding anything permanent, and publish the results whatever they showed.

The council has not yet agreed. Some members want to scrap the paper sheet for good, arguing that two systems cost more than one. Others point out that the app's gains are easy to count, while the harm to people who cannot use it is not recorded anywhere. For now Marlow runs both, and the argument continues.
