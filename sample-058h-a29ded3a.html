The board she did not try to fill

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original practice prose based on a classic puzzle. The puzzle and proof are not original. Classic puzzle and proof: John McCarthy, Stanford.

In an imagined workshop, Leela is asked to cover a board of eight rows and eight columns with rectangular pieces. Each piece covers exactly two squares sharing a side; pieces cannot overlap, leave gaps or extend beyond the board. Before she begins, two diagonally opposite corner squares are cut away. Sixty-two squares remain, and thirty-one pieces have been supplied. Her colleague Arun says the quantities fit, so a sufficiently clever arrangement must exist.

Leela does not start arranging. She colours the original board black and white like a chessboard, with neighbouring squares of opposite colours. Any permitted piece must cover one black square and one white square. Consequently, any collection of such pieces covers equal numbers of both colours, regardless of where the pieces lie or how they are turned. Rotating a piece changes its direction, not that equality.

The two missing corners were both black. The altered board therefore has thirty black squares and thirty-two white ones. No arrangement of pieces that cover equal numbers can fill those unequal totals. Arun's count establishes something necessary: a board covered by two-square pieces must have an even number of squares. It does not establish something sufficient. Having the right total does not guarantee having it in a form the pieces can supply.

Suppose Leela instead tried a thousand arrangements and found a gap each time. That record would describe a thousand failures, not show that every possible arrangement fails. Her colour argument reaches further because it identifies a feature preserved by every permitted move. Nor does the colouring alter the physical problem. The same obstruction exists on an unpainted board; the colours make it legible.

There is a limit to the result. A different damaged board with equal colour totals might still be impossible to cover for another reason. Leela has found a decisive obstruction, not a complete test for every board. What ends this search is not exhaustion but a reason why rearranging the same pieces cannot help.
