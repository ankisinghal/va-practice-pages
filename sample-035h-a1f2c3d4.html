The turns at Tendwa

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Every October the fields around Tendwa are watered by turns written on a wooden board in the panchayat veranda, a list older than the concrete canal itself. It named every holding, its day and its share, and the canal's gates were opened and shut to that list by whoever kept the keys that season. When Rhea, an irrigation engineer posted to the district, first saw the board she assumed it was a courtesy: tradition honoured in form, superseded in fact.

She spent a season measuring. Flow at the headworks, seepage along the laterals, the walking time between gates. She logged it all in a field book that grew heavier each week. Her models said each holding should receive water for a number of minutes proportional to its area and distance from the canal. The old turns, allotted in "the time a brass lota takes to empty," matched her computed times to within three minutes in forty-one of the forty-three holdings.

The two exceptions taught her more than the forty-one. The field called Bhoora received barely half of what her model allowed, and Khairi received a third more. The board's keeper, an old farmer named Sukhilal, could not explain in her vocabulary: Bhoora's soil "tired quickly" and Khairi's "drank and stayed thirsty." A soil survey that winter showed why: Bhoora's patch drains poorly over shallow saline groundwater, so extra watering waterlogs it and capillary action wicks salt up toward the roots, while Khairi's sandy lens drains before the crop is fed. The turns had been encoding that difference for generations before anyone wrote it down.

Rhea's report to the district recommended keeping the board and appending her tables to it, not replacing it. The board, she wrote, was a database that happened to be written in wood, and its homely units were a memory device rather than a measurement error. The engineer who succeeds her, she noted, will inherit both, and should learn the lota before the spreadsheet.
