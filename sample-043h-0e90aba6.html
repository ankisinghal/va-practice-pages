The frame with the blue ribbon

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Leela kept one frame in every beehive marked with a blue ribbon. The ribbon did not mean the frame was diseased, reserved for sale or part of an experiment. It meant only that she would not remove that frame during an inspection. Her new apprentice, Kabir, called the habit a superstition. Leela answered that it was closer to a fence than a charm: the mark stopped her from treating every useful thing as available simply because she could reach it.

Kabir objected that the bees knew nothing about ribbons, so the mark could not protect them. Leela agreed. She was not claiming that the ribbon improved the honey, guided the queen or changed the colony. It was for the keeper, not the bees. After each season she moved it to another frame, because a limit fixed forever to one object could become a rule about that object rather than a practice of restraint. The ribbon mattered only if it could be moved without being abandoned.

The test came during a dry season, when the hives yielded less than expected. Kabir suggested opening the ribboned frames just once. Leela refused. If the boundary disappeared whenever it cost something, she said, it had never been a boundary; it had been decoration. In spring, he noticed that one formerly ribboned frame was especially full and said the practice had protected that colony. Leela pointed to a neighbouring hive where every frame had been harvested and the spring stores were just as strong. The full frame showed what had happened, not why.

By then it was Kabir who asked whether the ribbon should stay on the same frame. Leela moved it to another one and gave him the task of checking that the mark still meant the same thing after the move. The practice continued without a claim that it increased yield or saved a hive. The ribbon did not tell Leela what the bees owed her. It told her where her own reach was to stop, even when the reason to reach farther was easy to name.
