The life beneath the field

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

A handful of healthy soil holds more living things than there are people in a large city. Most are too small to see: bacteria, fungi and tiny worms that spend their lives breaking down dead leaves and roots. Farmers have long known that such soil grows better crops, but only recently have scientists begun to measure how much of that work depends on the creatures underground.

Plants gain a good deal from this unseen labour. As the organisms feed, they release nutrients in forms that roots can absorb, and their tunnels let air and water move down through the earth. Some fungi even attach themselves to roots and trade minerals for sugar, a partnership that may be older than flowers.

The trouble is that this life is easily harmed. Heavy ploughing breaks up fungal threads, and chemical fertilisers can lead plants to depend less on their underground partners. Yields may rise for a few seasons, so the damage goes unnoticed, but soil that has lost its living layer is slow to recover and often needs ever more fertiliser to produce the same crop.

Some farmers are therefore returning to older habits: leaving crop residue on the field, ploughing less and growing different plants in turn. These methods are slower and less dramatic. They are also a reminder that fertile land is not a resource to be consumed but a community to be kept.
