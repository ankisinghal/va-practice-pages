Mending together

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

When Dunmore's repair café opened in a church hall two winters ago, the organisers wanted only a place to mend toasters and torn coats. Volunteers with soldering irons and sewing machines sat at long tables, and visitors waited with broken objects in carrier bags. Within weeks the hall was full on Saturdays, and a waiting list had to be started.

The founder, Ilse Brandt, noticed that the repairs mattered less than the conversation around them. People who had never spoken to a neighbour stayed for an hour to watch a lamp come back to life. Yet she was careful about claiming too much. "We fix perhaps six items in ten," she said, "and the rest go home in the same bag." A visitor whose radio could not be saved still thanked the volunteers, which she found both touching and slightly puzzling.

Not everyone was convinced. A shopkeeper on the high street complained that free repairs undercut his own small workshop, and he asked whether a charity hall should compete with a business that pays rent. The organisers answered that they refuse any job a professional would normally charge for, and send such visitors to him. Whether this has brought him customers is unclear, since neither side has kept a count, and he remains unconvinced.

The council now wants to give the café a grant, but with a condition: it must report how many items it saves from the bin. Ms Brandt is wary. A figure is easy to collect, she says, but it would measure the least interesting thing the hall does, and volunteers might begin to chase numbers instead of welcoming whoever walks in. The grant has not been decided, and for now the café goes on mending whatever arrives.
