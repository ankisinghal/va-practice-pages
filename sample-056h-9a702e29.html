Before the envelope opens

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

The final turn of the invented tournament game Harbour offers Mira two moves. Banking ends her game with four points. Opening a sealed envelope gives her ten points if it contains a white card and none if it contains a black one. The organisers say either colour is equally likely, and she has no clue about this particular envelope. Mira opens it, finds black and loses. A spectator calls the move foolish: four points, he says, are better than none.

That comparison describes the outcome, not the choice Mira faced. Before opening, she was choosing between four certain points and an even chance of ten. If the aim were to collect the highest average score over many independent games, opening would yield an average of five points per game. Banking would yield four. A single black card cannot turn the arithmetic into a mistake.

But that arithmetic does not settle every contest. Suppose Mira's opponent has already finished with six points, and the tournament awards a prize for winning this game, with nothing for second place. Banking four guarantees defeat; opening offers a chance of victory. Now suppose the opponent finished with three. Banking guarantees victory, whereas opening needlessly puts it at risk. The envelope has not changed. What counts as success has.

Nor does the argument require players to be indifferent to risk. A player trying to protect a minimum score may reasonably choose certainty over a higher average. The point is that such a preference supplies an objective; it does not refute the calculation. Calling one move prudent without saying what it protects leaves the judgment incomplete.

To review Mira's decision, therefore, cover the revealed card before discussing her move. Write down the score she needed and the reward she was pursuing. Then uncover the card. It belongs in the record of what happened, but it cannot supply information she lacked when she chose. Good analysis keeps the envelope closed long enough to ask the right question.
