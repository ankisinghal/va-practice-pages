The hours behind the workshop order

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original fictional passage written for practice, so full text is included.

In an imagined workshop, Mira and Dev make prints and wooden frames. In one working day, Mira can make either six prints or three frames. Dev can make either four prints or one frame. Each worker can divide a day between the tasks, with output changing in proportion to time. Switching tasks takes no time, and the workshop has enough materials for every plan considered.

Mira is faster at both tasks. That fact might seem to settle the allocation: give her the work, and let Dev help wherever he can. But speed alone leaves out what each worker gives up. For Mira, making one frame uses time that could have produced two prints. For Dev, the same frame costs four prints. Frames take a larger bite out of Dev's alternative output.

Suppose both split the day equally between the tasks. Together they make five prints and two frames. Now keep the two-frame target but change the timetable. Mira spends two thirds of her day making the frames and the remaining third making two prints. Dev spends his whole day printing and makes four. The revised plan produces six prints and two frames: the frame total is unchanged, while one more print appears.

Nothing in this calculation proves that making frames is Mira's better paid or more enjoyable occupation. Nor does it establish that six prints and two frames would satisfy every possible order. The comparison holds a particular frame requirement fixed and asks what else the same working time can yield. If customers instead wanted only prints, assigning Mira any framing time would reduce the relevant output.

The workshop therefore draws two diagrams before accepting an order. One records each worker's possible outputs; the other divides the available hours. A customer's request connects them. Mira's greater speed remains a fact, but it is no longer expected to dictate every division of labour. The useful question is not who wins a contest at each task. It is which arrangement meets the request while giving up less of something else the workshop could make.
