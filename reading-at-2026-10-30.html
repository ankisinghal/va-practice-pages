The fair that graded the failures

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

For thirty years the science fair at Ashok Vidyalaya was the school's proudest evening, and for most of those years it looked the same. Volcano models fizzed on schedule, circuit boards blinked in neat rows, and the judges, three teachers with clipboards, rewarded whatever gleamed. Projects were polished, safe and, the physics master Mr Rao admitted, interchangeable. Nobody could remember a student attempting anything that might fail in public.

When Meera D'Souza took over as coordinator, she changed one line of the rubric: half the marks would now go to the project log, the dated record of guesses, trials, dead ends and revisions, and half to the exhibit itself. Several teachers objected. A log, they argued, rewards mess and punishes the student who gets things right quietly; neatness is a virtue too, and not every discipline leaves a paper trail.

The first fair under the new rubric was louder and stranger. One team exhibited a bridge that had collapsed four times before it stood, each failure taped into the log with its cause. Another brought a soil-moisture alarm and admitted, in writing, that three of their sensor placements had drowned. A traditionally immaculate project, a gleaming periscope assembled without a single recorded decision, scored well on craft and poorly overall, and its makers complained loudly.

The second year brought the deeper change. Students began ambitious work months earlier, because an ambitious failure documented in October was worth more than a safe success assembled in January. The winner was a quiet Class 9 student whose log recorded eleven wing designs before one glider flew the length of the corridor. The sceptics were not entirely wrong, though: a few students began decorating their logs with invented failures, so Meera added five-minute interviews in which judges asked entrants to explain two entries from their own pages.

The fair never proved that failure teaches. What it proved, at Ashok Vidyalaya, is that measuring something changes what people do. The rubric had always claimed to reward thinking; only the log made thinking visible enough to mark.
