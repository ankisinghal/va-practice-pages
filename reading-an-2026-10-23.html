The ink on Chandra Lane

Asha Vardhan's letterpress studio has occupied the same narrow shop on Chandra Lane for thirty-one years. The press itself, a cast-iron machine older than she is, prints wedding cards, theatre posters and, once a year, the lane's own festival programme. Neighbours say they can tell her work blindfolded: her type leaves a slight dent in the paper that no laser printer imitates. The shop has never needed a signboard; the sound of the press does the work of one.

Three years ago the studio nearly closed. Paper prices climbed, two long-standing clients moved their printing online, and Asha told the local newspaper she would sell the machine "to someone who will oil it". What changed, she says now, was not sentiment but arithmetic. She began running Saturday workshops for school groups at a modest fee, and the workshops, not the sympathy her story attracted, paid the rent. "People came to save the shop," she says. "The children who learned to set type are the reason it is still open."

Not everyone reads it that way. Her cousin Prakash, who sold his own bindery some years ago, argues the workshops are a distraction. Schools book them once, he says, take photographs and never return, while the studio actually survives on print orders from a loyal few customers who have bought from her for decades. Asha counters that a third of last year's adult students commissioned work later, though she admits she keeps no formal records.

On a recent Saturday, nine teenagers spent an hour choosing type for a line each of them had written. The oldest press on the lane printed their words crookedly, and nobody minded. A teacher from a school forty kilometres away has already asked whether Asha can take a second class in winter.
