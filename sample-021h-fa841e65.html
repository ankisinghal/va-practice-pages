The notebook at Hollin Farm

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

For forty years, Desmond Hale walked to the same fence post at Hollin Farm each morning to read a copper rain gauge and write the number in a green notebook. Nobody had asked him to. The village now had a modern weather station on the school roof, which sent its readings to a computer every ten minutes, and most neighbours assumed his habit was a hobby that had outlived its use.

Then the school's sensor began to drift. A technician found that its rain totals had crept upward, a little more each year, although no single reading looked wrong. It was Hale's notebook that exposed the fault. When the school's figures were laid beside his, the two agreed for the first ten years and then slowly parted, like two walkers who start in step but take slightly different strides.

The council was quick to praise the notebook, but Hale was uncomfortable with the attention. He pointed out that his gauge stood beside a hedge that had grown taller over the years, which probably made his totals a little low in recent winters. "A record is only as honest as its notes," he said, and he began adding a line about the hedge to his entries. Some villagers found the remark fussy. Priya Nandan, a retired engineer, thought it was the most useful sentence in the book.

The council now plans to retire the notebook and rely on the sensor alone, once it has been repaired. Hale does not object, but he has asked that the old pages be kept. The sensor, he says, is excellent at telling you what the rain did, and a notebook is better at telling you what else was happening around it. The council has not yet answered his request.
