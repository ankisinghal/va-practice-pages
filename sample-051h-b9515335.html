The Friday calls of the bazaar

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Every Friday for forty-one years, Gafur Din climbed the wooden steps above Chur Bazaar at five in the evening and called out the week's notices in Shina, the old market language of the valley: which bridge would close for repairs, whose wheat had arrived, when the vaccination team would come. Shopkeepers answered in the same language, bargaining across the square without leaving their stalls.

Over the last few years, as phones reached the valley, the notices quietly moved to a message group. The wheat arrivals, the bridge repairs and the vaccination dates all arrived as short typed lines in the standard language. Nothing was announced; nobody objected. But Gafur noticed smaller things. Fewer people gathered near the steps to hear him. The shopkeepers still traded, yet the quick replies, the jokes and the shouted corrections when he misread a name faded within a season.

Rani, a schoolteacher who had grown up answering from her grandfather's stall, asked the group's administrator to save Gafur's old call sheets so she could archive them. He offered her the typed notices instead, which she declined. Those were the notices, she said, not the language. The calls were never a way of displaying information; they were a way of doing business, and half their meaning lived in the answers from the square.

Saving the sheets was easy, and she did it. But keeping a record, she argued, is not keeping a custom. So she and three stallholders arranged that on the first Friday of every month, Gafur would call the notices from the steps again, with schoolchildren standing beside him, learning the calls by answering them.

The first such Friday, the square was louder than it had been in a year. The message group still carries the weekday notices, typed and efficient. But once a month the valley hears the difference between remembering a language and using one.
