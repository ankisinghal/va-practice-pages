The ballot without symbols

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Three years ago the hill province of Kandara changed its municipal ballots in two ways: party symbols no longer appeared beside candidates' names, and the deposit required to stand for election was cut to a token sum. In the first elections held under the new rules, incumbent councillors kept their seats in barely half of races, down from about seven in ten, and reformers claimed vindication. Freed from the party label, they argued, voters had at last weighed each candidate's actual record.

The reasoning behind that claim is that a symbol is a shorthand. A voter who knows a party's platform can mark its candidate without knowing anything about the person, and incumbents ride on their party's reputation even when their own work has been poor. Remove the symbol, the argument runs, and the voter must judge the individual or choose blindly.

Sceptics offer a colder account. The cheaper deposit, they note, drew crowds of first-time candidates, and ballots that once carried two or three names now carried six or seven. Incumbents may have lost not because voters judged them but because support scattered among challengers. On that reading, the reform changed arithmetic, not attitudes.

The pattern inside the results favours the reformers. Losses climbed even in the minority of wards where only two names appeared, and there no scattering can be blamed; incumbents in such wards fell almost as often as those facing crowded ballots. The first-time winners in those wards had typically campaigned on street-level grievances such as water supply, drains and clinic hours rather than on party themes.

A cleaner test is coming. The deposit has now been restored to its old level, so entry is no longer easier than it was before the reform, while the symbols remain off the ballot. If incumbents keep falling at the new rate, the judgment account survives its hardest challenge; if they recover, the sceptics will be owed an apology.
