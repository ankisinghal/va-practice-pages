When a measure becomes a target

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

A hospital wishes to reduce waiting times and announces that no patient should wait more than four hours. Soon the figures improve. Yet some patients report that little has changed: they now spend their four hours in a corridor, officially "seen" by a nurse who has done no more than take their name, before the real wait begins.

This is an old problem. Once a measure becomes a target, people learn to satisfy the number rather than the purpose it was meant to serve. The behaviour is rarely dishonest in any crude sense. Staff under pressure respond sensibly to what is rewarded, and the measure, which began as a window onto quality, becomes a wall in front of it.

None of this proves that measurement is useless. Without numbers, improvement would rest on impressions, and impressions favour the loudest voice. The difficulty lies in treating any single figure as the thing itself. A sensible approach uses several measures that are hard to satisfy together, and keeps asking whether the numbers still describe what matters.

In short, a good measure serves its purpose until it is made the goal; then it must be watched, balanced and, from time to time, replaced.
