The notice board at Beldih High

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

In January, Beldih High School stopped reading notices aloud at morning assembly and began printing a single daily bulletin, pinned to a board outside the office. The principal, Mr Awasthi, reckoned the change would hand ten quiet minutes back to every class and teach students to fetch information for themselves rather than wait to be told.

By March the complaints had reached him. The debating society's trials drew half their usual numbers, two teams travelled to fixtures without their reserves, and the staff room settled on an easy explanation: the students had grown careless. Mr Awasthi agreed, and spoke sternly about responsibility at the next assembly.

A new teacher, Ms Rhea, read the same term differently. The bulletin had not failed, she argued, because students were careless; it failed because it asked for a habit they had never needed. Notices once arrived in their ears whether they listened or not. Now information waited in one corner of the campus, and only students with a reason to pass that corner would ever meet it.

She proposed a month's experiment. Duplicate boards went up where students already gathered, by the canteen hatch and on the library stairs, while official notices stayed where they were. By the end of the month, club trials and fixtures were well attended again, and teachers reported fewer bewildered faces. Yet fee deadlines and examination forms, announced only on the boards, were still missed at exactly the old rate.

The school kept the canteen and library boards and quietly returned genuinely official announcements to a two-minute spoken slot. Mr Awasthi told the staff he had learned something about attention: students looked at boards for what interested them, not for what the office considered urgent. A notice, the term had shown, reaches people where their attention already lives; anything truly official still has to be pushed.
