The judgment between the words

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original fictional passage written for practice, so full text is included.

In an imagined translation workshop, Nalin is asked to translate a short story whose narrator calls a neighbour 'a generous man' immediately after describing his refusal to lend a candle. Nalin's first version replaces the phrase with 'a miser'. It conveys what he thinks the narrator means. His second preserves the praise. A reader unfamiliar with the narrator may initially take that praise literally. Which version has translated the story more faithfully?

The question cannot be settled by consulting a dictionary alone. A dictionary can establish that 'generous' does not ordinarily mean 'miserly', but irony depends on the distance between a speaker's words and the judgment a reader constructs. Replacing the words with the inferred judgment may therefore preserve a conclusion while removing the activity through which the story invites its reader to reach it.

This is not an argument for copying every expression mechanically. Imagine that the translated phrase is a conventional insult in the receiving language, so that nobody could hear it as praise. Keeping that phrase would not preserve the original tension; it would eliminate one of its necessary possibilities. A translator might need different words to make both readings available. Verbal similarity and similarity of experience can pull in opposite directions.

Nor must every reader finally choose the same interpretation. Perhaps the neighbour has been generous elsewhere, or perhaps the narrator's complaint is itself unreasonable. If the story leaves those possibilities open, a translation that settles them through an explanatory substitution claims an authority the story withholds. A note may identify a relevant custom without announcing which character deserves our sympathy.

Nalin consequently marks two separate questions beside his drafts: what judgment does this wording suggest, and what room does it leave for the reader to resist that judgment? His task is not to hide every difficulty or preserve every difficulty unchanged. It is to distinguish an obstacle created accidentally by translation from an uncertainty the story deliberately asks the reader to inhabit.
