The chess clocks of Ridgemont

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Five years ago, the chess club at Ridgemont School was eight students around two boards in a borrowed classroom. This spring its senior team won the regional schools league for the third consecutive year, and more than sixty students now attend the Thursday sessions, from nine-year-olds learning notation to the five seniors who hold the league trophy.

Farida Nayar, the teacher who founded the club, credits one habit. Every Friday, each player brings a game they lost that week, and the group reconstructs it move by move on a demonstration board. "We do not celebrate the person who won that game," she says. "We interrogate it." She believes this ritual of shared analysis, more than talent, equipment or funding, explains the club's rise.

The school has supported the ritual with what Nayar calls meagre means: second-hand clocks, donated sets, a cupboard that serves as the library. Some parents observe that three of the five senior players arrived already ranked, transferring from a neighbouring school after it closed its own programme. Nayar does not dispute the fact, only its weight: rankings, she answers, do not play matches, and the same three players lost regularly before they joined Ridgemont.

A league official who has watched the club for a decade offers a different account. Most schools, she points out, rebuild their teams every two years as students graduate, while Ridgemont's juniors train beside the seniors from the age of nine, so experience is handed down rather than lost. Whatever the true cause, imitation has begun. Two neighbouring schools have started their own loss-analysis Fridays, though early results, the official notes with a smile, have been mixed.

Nayar is untroubled by the imitators. "A habit is not a formula," she says. "You cannot photocopy a culture."
