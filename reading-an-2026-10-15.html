The table of replicas

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Until last spring, the Ravenel Museum kept its collection behind glass. Visitors could read labels and peer at Roman coins, clay lamps and a worn leather shoe, but nothing could be touched. Staff had long worried that the cases made the past feel distant, something to be admired from across the room rather than understood. Then the curator, Tamsin Ormerod, set up a table of replicas that anyone could pick up, turn over and weigh in their hands.

The table was an immediate success. Children queued to try on a copy of the shoe, and school groups asked to return. Ormerod credits the replicas for the museum's busiest summer, pointing out that attendance rose by a third. Yet she is careful not to claim that touch is better than looking. "A replica tells you how a thing felt," she says, "but only the original tells you that someone really made it."

Some trustees were uneasy. One argued that a museum earns trust by guarding rare objects, and that handling copies might teach visitors to treat the real ones carelessly. Ormerod answered that the glass cases are staying, and that the table sits in a different room so the two are never confused. Whether the table changes how visitors behave near the originals has not been studied, and she admits it may take years to find out.

The trustees now want the table to pay for itself, either through a small entry fee or a shop selling replicas. Ormerod is reluctant. A charge, she fears, would turn a place of curiosity into a place of buying, and the families who most enjoy the table may be the ones least able to pay. She would rather look for other ways to raise money, such as small grants from local businesses, though she admits none of them is certain to cover the costs. The trustees have not yet decided.
