The pass at the Phulbari depot

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

The Phulbari bus depot introduced a monthly pass in April: unlimited rides on the town's four routes for the price of eighteen single tickets. The union council called it a way to fill half-empty buses, and by June the depot had sold nine hundred passes, mostly to mill workers and students.

Then the accounts clerk noticed something odd. Ticket-window revenue had fallen by almost a third, although the buses were fuller than ever. The depot manager, Mr Tolani, declared the pass a slow leak: regulars who once paid for twenty-two rides a month now rode thirty times for the price of eighteen. At this rate, he said, the pass would eat the depot's surplus by winter, and he asked the council to suspend it.

Sunita, the young operations assistant, read the same ledger differently. The missing revenue, she argued, had not vanished into the passes; most of it had never existed. Many pass holders were new riders - students who used to walk, mill hands who once shared cycle-rickshaws - and their eighteen-ticket price was money the depot had never seen before. The window was quieter precisely because regulars no longer needed it.

The renewal figures in October gave her reading its weight. Nearly all nine hundred pass holders renewed, and four hundred new passes were sold, most of them to first-time riders from the new housing colony. Window revenue stayed low, but total receipts, passes plus tickets, were the highest in the depot's records. The supposed leak had been a tap.

The council voted to keep the pass, and Mr Tolani, to his credit, seconded the motion. The episode is now cited at the depot whenever a familiar queue goes quiet: a shorter line at the window is not always a smaller business.
