The diary with one wrong line

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

For sixty years the town of Velapur has read the Battle Diary of Keshav Rao aloud in the fort courtyard every October. The diary, twelve pages of hurried ink, describes the 1862 defence of the town's granary and is the closest thing Velapur has to a founding document. When Meenakshi Iyer, a young archivist cataloguing the district museum's papers, declared that the diary could not have been written during the battle, the town treated the news as a small earthquake.

Her case rests on one line. The diary says the defenders could hear 'the whistle from the new railway station' as they barred the granary door. Velapur's station, Meenakshi points out, was opened twelve years after the battle; no defender could have heard a whistle from a building that did not exist. A contemporary account, she argues, cannot contain an anachronism, so the diary must be a later composition - a pious forgery, she calls it: invented later, but invented to honour, not to deceive.

The museum's trustees answer with the weather. The diary's description of a late, violent monsoon that October matches the district's rainfall records exactly, they say, and a later writer could not have known the storm. Meenakshi replies that the records were published in a gazetteer forty years ago, available to any careful fabricator. The trustees then point to the paper, a laid stock the town's mill stopped making in 1870, and the argument settles into a polite stalemate of ink and fibre.

The stalemate broke this spring, when ultraviolet imaging showed the station line in a different hand from the rest of the page - a marginal gloss, squeezed in by a later copyist, which had migrated into the main text in the bound copy the town knows. The diary beneath the gloss appears to be a real contemporary account, copied once and annotated once. Velapur will go on reading it aloud in October, Meenakshi says, and it is now her voice that reads the line about the whistle, with a pause the town has learned to listen for.
