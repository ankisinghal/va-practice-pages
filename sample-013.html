Familiar is not the same as known

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Most students are told to revise by reading their notes again and again. It feels productive: the page looks familiar, and the familiarity is mistaken for knowledge. Teachers who study learning have noticed the trap. Recognising an answer when you see it is much easier than producing it from memory on an empty page.

A better habit is to close the book and try to recall what you have just read. The attempt is uncomfortable, and the first tries are often poor. Yet this effort is what strengthens memory, because the mind has to rebuild the idea instead of simply looking at it. Spreading such practice over several days works better than a single long evening before an exam.

None of this means that rereading is useless. A quick second look can fix a misunderstanding or fill a gap found during recall. The mistake is to make it the whole method. A student who tests herself, checks the notes only for what she missed, and returns to the topic a few days later is doing less comfortable work, but is far more likely to remember it.

The lesson is that feeling confident and being prepared are different things, and only the second one counts in the exam hall.
