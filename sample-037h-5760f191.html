The street that gave back its stream

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

For forty years the monsoon stream that crosses Kesarpur ran out of sight, piped into a concrete drain and paved over. The shopkeepers of Station Road called the drain the town's one unarguable success: no flooded storerooms, no mosquitoes, and forty extra parking spaces on a street that needed every one. When the district's new engineer, Farah Qureshi, proposed digging up a block and letting the stream run in the open again, the traders' association objected within the hour.

Farah's case was not nostalgia. The drain was cracking along its length, she told the council, and rebuilding it would cost nearly as much as reopening the channel; a shallow, planted stream could spread and slow a heavy monsoon instead of choking on it. The association answered that an open channel would flood worse, not better, and that losing parking, even for a single season, would finish shops that one bad Diwali had already weakened.

The council settled on an interim arrangement: one block, one monsoon, then a public review. The trial monsoon was not gentle with the experiment. Volunteers dredged silt from the channel twice, a bakery lost a morning to mopping, and the association printed the photographs. But the stretch of drain just below the trial block, which had overflowed in each of the last three monsoons, did not overflow at all.

The quieter changes took longer to notice. Evening walkers began choosing the open block, two tea stalls extended their hours, and a school adopted the channel for its water-testing project. The association's grudging review conceded the drainage point. The parking complaint, though, had not been solved, as everyone admitted; it had only been moved two lanes away, where it sat unresolved.

Kesarpur has not proved that buried streams should be opened everywhere; another town's soil, slope and trade would argue differently. What the trial block proved is smaller and, the council thinks, more useful: a contested claim about a whole town can be tested on a single block, cheaply, before anyone has to win the argument outright.
