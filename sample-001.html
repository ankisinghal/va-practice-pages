Why plans run late

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 3 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Ask a student how long an essay will take and the answer is usually confident: two hours, perhaps three. Ask the same student afterwards, and the real figure is often double. Psychologists call this habit the planning fallacy, and what makes it interesting is that experience does not seem to cure it.

One explanation is that we plan by picturing the best version of events. When we imagine writing the essay, we see ourselves sitting down, finding the sources at once and typing smoothly. We rarely imagine the lost file, the argument that collapses halfway or the friend who calls. Each interruption is unlikely on its own, but there are so many possible interruptions that some of them nearly always happen. The plan is a story about one tidy path; the work takes place on a messy one.

A second explanation concerns memory. After a task is finished, we tend to remember how it ended rather than how long it dragged. The relief of finishing smooths the memory, so the next estimate starts from a flattering past. People are not lying to others when they give a short estimate; they are sincerely relying on a record that has been edited without their noticing.

What helps? Some researchers suggest looking outward rather than inward: instead of asking how long this essay will take, ask how long essays like it have taken. The answer is less exciting but usually more accurate, because it includes the delays that imagination leaves out. Others advise splitting a task into small steps and estimating each one, which forces the hidden chores into view. Neither method removes the bias entirely, and a person who is already anxious may simply pad every estimate until planning becomes useless.

The lesson is not that people are careless. It is that a feeling of confidence is a poor measure of a plan's quality. A plan deserves trust when it has met the evidence of past delays, not merely when it feels convincing.
