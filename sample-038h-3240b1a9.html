The librarian's quiet index

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

Every December the town library in Sahpur publishes its most-borrowed list, and every December the same exam-preparation guide tops it. Meera D'Souza, who has staffed the returns counter for nineteen years, keeps a different list in a drawer beside her desk: the books that come back late, annotated, dog-eared, sometimes with bus tickets or pressed leaves left between the pages. Her list runs on no software, only on returns, and it tells a different story about what the town actually loves.

The checkout system, Meera says, counts convenience, not attachment. The exam guide is borrowed because it is assigned, sold out in the shops, and free at the library; nobody writes in it, and nobody renews it once the results are out. The poetry collection that sits sixteenth on the computer's ranking comes back slowly, but it comes back changed: lines starred in pencil, arguments carried on in the margins in two different hands, a recipe pressed flat at page forty. A book that is loved, she argues, is handled differently from a book that is merely needed.

Meera admits her index has its own distortions. A heavy hardback is rarely carried to the bus stop, however fond its reader is, and a cheap paperback can be loved, worn out, and replaced rather than returned. The computer sees none of this either way. When the head of the district libraries visited and praised Sahpur's circulation figures, Meera answered with what she calls the books' quiet referendum: 'Votes are cast in pencil, not at the counter.'

This winter she spent the modest buying budget from the drawer rather than the database: second copies of the poetry collection and of the field guide whose spine always fails, and fewer of the guide that tops the list. The most-borrowed table went up on the noticeboard as usual. Meera's list stayed in the drawer, where it has now outlasted three library software systems.
