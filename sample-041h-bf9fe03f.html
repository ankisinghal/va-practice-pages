The tide bell on the new map

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

When Nila updated the digital map of Harran Creek, the old ferry disappeared. The crossing was little more than a platform on oil drums, pulled along a rope by two fishermen, so the satellite layer read it as debris. The map sent walkers to a bridge forty minutes away. At high tide, however, a queue still formed beside the water whenever the ferryman's brass bell rang. Nila's first note in the map's comment box was blunt: the ferry was not a landmark, but a route.

She restored the ferry by hand and expected the queue to vanish from the bridge path. It did not. The fishermen still waited for the bell before casting off, and walkers who arrived between rings turned back to the bridge. Nila assumed habit was slowing adoption. The ferryman corrected her: the bell marked a safe current, not a timetable. Without knowing whether the bell had rung, the map could show where the ferry was, yet not whether crossing was sensible.

For the next two weeks, the ferryman sent Nila short reports after each ring: current steady, debris near the rope, crossing paused, or all clear. The map's ferry suggestions grew less misleading, and fewer walkers were sent to the water when the crossing was still. Nila concluded that the daily notes had made the map reliable. Her colleague Sajan urged caution. During the same fortnight, bridge repairs had ended and the heaviest rain had stopped. Either change could have reduced bad journeys without the notes doing any work.

Nila checked the records and found that the notes helped most on unsettled days, when conditions changed faster than the map's scheduled updates. She therefore stopped treating the bell as an old object to be replaced. Its ring became a live signal in the map, while the ferry icon carried the words 'crossing reported' only after a fresh note. When no report arrived, the map showed the ferry's location but not a promise. The new map did not defeat the tide bell. It finally admitted what the bell had been measuring.
