The questions given away early

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

When Meera took charge of the physics department at Haldane College, she did what her predecessor had refused to do for thirty years: at the start of term she published the entire final-exam question bank, all six hundred questions, free for anyone to download. Critics inside the college called it a surrender. Students would simply memorise the answers, they said, and the examination would stop measuring anything at all.

Her reply rested on a single argument. A question that can be answered from memory alone was never measuring understanding in the first place. Publishing the bank would force her faculty to write questions that asked students to apply ideas to unfamiliar situations, because any question with a rehearsed answer would be worthless by design.

Two years later, the results refused to satisfy either side completely. Average scores rose, but only slightly, and the gap between the strongest and weakest students widened rather than narrowed. Failure rates barely moved. What clearly changed was how students studied: they formed groups to argue over the published questions, and tutors reported fewer requests for leaked papers and more requests for help with method.

The critics were not entirely wrong. An internal review found that about a fifth of the bank had quietly hardened into pattern drills: the numbers changed from year to year, the reasoning did not, and diligent memorisers could still do well on those items. The review also noted that the weakest students were the most likely to rely on that route, precisely because it asked less of them.

Meera did not withdraw the bank. She now describes it as the department's public statement of what it means to know physics, and she holds her faculty to that statement: if a question can be gamed, the fault lies with the question, not with the student who games it. The bank, she says, is less a gift to students than a standard the college has agreed to be judged by.
