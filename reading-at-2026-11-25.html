The moment both can see

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original fictional passage written for practice, so full text is included.

In an imagined rehearsal, two stagehands stand at opposite ends of a curtain. The curtain opens properly only if both release their ropes on the same beat. Each can see a lamp above the stage, and each knows that the other can see it. The director has agreed with both that a flash will be their shared signal. Until it comes, neither should release a rope.

Before the rehearsal, the director separately tells each stagehand that the equipment is ready. Both messages arrive, and both stagehands believe them. Yet the curtain stays closed. Readiness is not the missing information. Neither stagehand knows whether the other has received a message or is about to act. Releasing alone would spoil the opening, so waiting remains consistent with believing the director.

The lamp then flashes. It adds no new fact about the equipment. Instead, it makes the occasion for acting visible to both at once. Each sees the flash and can see that the other has an unobstructed view of it. Their agreement connects that shared observation to the same action. They release together. The flash matters because of what they can reasonably expect of one another, not because it carries a longer or more accurate report.

This would not work merely because a lamp happened to be bright. If one stagehand thought the other could not see it, the uncertainty would return. Nor would a visible flash coordinate people who had agreed to different responses. The physical event, their awareness of its shared visibility, and the prior agreement do different jobs. Together they make the flash usable as a signal.

Afterwards, the director leaves the separate readiness messages in place. They tell each stagehand whether the equipment can be used. For the opening itself, she keeps the shared flash. The two forms of communication are not rivals in a contest over truth. One supplies a fact; the other gives people who already accept that fact a common occasion to move. On the next run, both hands remain on the ropes until the lamp lights.
