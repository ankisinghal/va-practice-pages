The wage that changed the weave

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

When the Mahua weavers' cooperative switched from paying members per metre of cloth to paying them per hour at the loom, its secretary, Devika, expected output to fall. The cooperative, which sold cloth to a few regular buyers in the city, had paid by the metre for thirty years. Piece rates had rewarded speed, and an hourly wage seemed to remove the reason to hurry. The members were divided: older weavers welcomed a wage that did not punish a difficult pattern, while younger ones suspected the change would mostly benefit slower colleagues.

By the end of the first quarter, the cooperative's rejected-cloth rate had halved. Devika told the annual meeting that hourly pay had improved quality because weavers could now stop to correct a flaw without losing earnings; under the old system a flawed piece was returned unpaid, so a fault found late cost a weaver a day's pay. Several members were persuaded, and one proposed extending the hourly system to the dyeing shed as well.

Rohit, an accountant asked to review the books, put a different question to the meeting: what had the cooperative paid for each accepted metre? Output had fallen by about a tenth. With fewer pieces rejected but more hours paid, the cost of each accepted metre had barely moved. Devika agreed that the figures were correct, but she said cost per metre was not the only fair measure of a change.

Devika added that two of the buyers had remarked on how even the cloth now looked. Her point was that members who once hid flaws to protect their pay now reported them at the loom. Rohit accepted that this was a real gain, though one the books could not price. He asked only that the cooperative stop describing the change as having paid for itself. The meeting voted to keep hourly pay for weaving and to leave the dyeing shed as it was for another year.
