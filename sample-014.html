The archive and the missing voices

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

In an imagined town, a new archivist is asked to prepare an exhibition about a bridge built eighty years earlier. The official minutes celebrate the mayor who approved the project. A contractor's ledger lists materials and wages. Letters from nearby shopkeepers complain about dust, blocked roads and lost customers. These papers describe the same bridge, but they do not tell the same story.

The archivist first considers placing the minutes beside a photograph of the finished bridge. It would make a tidy display: a decision, followed by an achievement. Yet the shopkeepers' letters disturb that neat sequence. They do not prove that the bridge was a mistake. They show that a project remembered as a public success could have imposed private costs while it was being built.

She therefore arranges the documents around a question: who could record their experience, and whose experience has survived? There are no letters from the labourers named in the ledger. Their silence in the collection is not evidence that they were content. It is a limit on what the exhibition can claim.

The final display honours the bridge without turning its construction into an uncomplicated triumph. The archivist has not replaced celebration with accusation. She has made room for uncertainty, allowing visitors to see both what the records reveal and what they leave unresolved.
