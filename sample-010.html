Words that travel

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

English is often described as a borrower. Walk through a market in any Indian city and you will hear the evidence: shampoo, jungle, bungalow and pyjamas all began as words from South Asian languages before turning up in English dictionaries. The same is true in the other direction, as "table", "station" and "school" now sit comfortably inside Hindi, Tamil and Bengali speech.

Such borrowing is usually driven by need rather than prestige. When traders, soldiers or sailors meet something that has no name in their own language, the quickest solution is to take the word that goes with the object. Over time the word is bent to fit the new language: its sound shifts, its spelling settles, and the speakers forget it was ever foreign.

Purists often object, warning that a language filled with borrowed words loses its character. History suggests otherwise. Languages that have absorbed the most from others, English among them, tend to be the ones with the largest vocabularies and the most flexible ways of expression. A language, it seems, is strengthened by contact more than weakened by it.

None of this means that every import is an improvement. Some borrowed words replace perfectly good native ones for no reason beyond fashion. But the process itself is as old as speech, and it is hard to stop.
