A shorter list is not a winner

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original fictional passage written for practice, so full text is included.

In an imagined town, Mira draws three walking routes between a station and a theatre. Birch takes twelve minutes and has four flights of stairs. Canal takes fifteen minutes and has no stairs. Market takes eighteen minutes and has two flights of stairs. All are equally safe and sheltered. Mira is asked to recommend the best route, but first crosses Market off her list.

Her reason is not that everyone values time more than climbing. Canal is both quicker than Market and free of its stairs. Anyone who wants a shorter walk and fewer stairs has no reason, on the stated information, to choose Market over Canal. The comparison removes an option without requiring Mira to decide how many minutes a flight of stairs is worth.

Birch and Canal present a different problem. Birch saves three minutes, while Canal avoids four flights. A traveller with a painful knee may choose Canal; someone hurrying to collect a ticket may choose Birch. The first comparison did not supply a scale for making this second choice. Eliminating an inferior option is not the same achievement as identifying a uniquely best remaining one.

A numerical score can conceal that difference. Suppose Mira awards one point for every minute saved and deducts one point for each flight climbed. Canal then beats Birch. But the arithmetic has not discovered the relative importance of walking time and stairs. It has put a particular exchange rate into the calculation. A traveller who objects to that rate is not necessarily making an error in addition.

The crossed-out route deserves another look if the description is incomplete. Suppose Market alone passes a pharmacy where the traveller must collect medicine. Canal's advantages no longer settle that person's choice, because an omitted requirement has entered the comparison. Mira should therefore be firm about the limited conclusion her list supports: Market loses when only time and stairs matter. She can narrow a decision without pretending to finish it. Before adding a more elaborate score, she should ask whether she has listed the things that actually matter to this traveller.
