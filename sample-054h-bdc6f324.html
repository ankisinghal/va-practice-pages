Two ways to count a forest

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

For thirty years, the forest above Cheur was counted on foot. Every April, walkers followed the same three transects in the midmorning and wrote down every bird they saw or heard. This year the forest committee set up acoustic recorders instead, and the new count came back forty per cent higher than last April's. The town paper ran the story as a recovery: "The birds are back."

Ila Dhar, a field biologist who has walked the transects for twelve years, read the numbers differently. The recorders and the walkers, she pointed out, do not count the same birds. Recorders listen all night and catch the shy canopy singers that call at dawn and dusk and are almost never seen. Walkers, moving under the trees by day, are good at exactly the birds the recorders miss: ground-feeding birds that rarely call, and raptors circling high above the microphones.

The new total is not fake. The canopy singers were always there; the walking counts simply undercounted them, and the recorders have made the undercount visible. But an undercount corrected is not a population recovered. When Dhar walked the old transects herself this April and compared like with like, several of the silent ground species were in fact down, while no canopy species could be shown to be up.

The distinction matters because the committee is about to choose between three-year plans on the strength of the headline number. If the rise is mostly a change in what gets counted, then reading it as a trend manufactures good news and could steer money away from the ground species that are genuinely thinning.

Her recommendation was narrow. Keep the recorders, because they hear what walkers cannot, and keep one walked transect, because it sees what recorders cannot. But publish the two counts as two series with two names, never as one rising line. A graph that climbs only because the ruler changed does not say the forest grew.
