Who gets the road

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

When a mid-sized city introduced a congestion charge for its centre, supporters expected the argument to be about traffic. It turned out to be about something older: whether a price is a fair way to decide who may use a shared space.

Defenders of the charge made a practical case. Roads are scarce, they said, and scarcity needs some form of rationing. A price at least lets drivers weigh their own needs, so that a delivery van with a deadline can pay for speed while a casual trip can wait. Queues and permits, the alternatives, ration just as surely, and often reward whoever has time to wait or contacts to ask.

Critics did not deny that the charge reduced traffic. Their objection was that the reduction was achieved unevenly. A fee that a wealthy household barely notices can change a poorer household's week, and so the same road is effectively open to some residents and closed to others. To call the outcome efficient, they argued, is to treat willingness to pay as if it were the same as need.

The council's reply was to earmark the revenue for cheaper bus fares and to exempt night-shift workers. This softened the objection without removing it. Exemptions require someone to decide who qualifies, and each category invites disputes about the boundary. A household just outside it can reasonably ask why its need counts for less.

What the episode suggests is not that prices are unfair, or that they are fair, but that the answer depends on what accompanies them. A charge paired with real alternatives and visible protection for those least able to pay is a different policy from the same charge imposed alone, even though the number on the sign is identical.
