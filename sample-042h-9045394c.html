The seat without the numbers

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

At the Kolar Rowing Club, every practice began with numbers. From the stroke seat, Ishaan called the crew's split after each five hundred metres: a little down, a little up, then the same again. The calls helped the eight find a pace, but Tara, newly moved from sculling, noticed that one slow call changed the boat before anyone had seen the next stretch of water. Shoulders rose, blades hurried, and the crew spent the following strokes correcting a mistake that might already have passed.

During a wet week before the inter-club regatta, coach Rehan gave Tara a 'blind seat' turn. She would hear only the rhythm of the oars, while Ishaan kept the split watch and made no calls. The arrangement was not a rejection of measurement. Tara still wrote the numbers down after landing, and the others rowed with their usual calls. Rehan said he wanted the crew to learn when a number deserved a response and when it merely reported disturbed water.

Ishaan objected that hiding information would make the crew dependent on guesswork. Tara answered that she was not hiding it; she was postponing it until the boat could compare feeling with fact. Their disagreement sharpened on the final practice run, when a crosswind roughened the first stretch. Tara held the rhythm without hearing the poor opening split, and the boat settled instead of chasing it. Ishaan later said the blind-seat turn had caused the steadier run. Rehan was more cautious: the crew had also shortened its warm-up, and the wind dropped after the first stretch.

At the regatta, Rehan did not silence Ishaan. He rotated the blind seat among the rowers and asked each to record one sentence before seeing the splits: what the boat felt like. The closing page of the training book therefore held two kinds of evidence side by side. The clock kept its authority over time, but not over every decision. The crew's new practice was not to row without numbers; it was to keep a number from becoming an instruction before anyone had judged it.
