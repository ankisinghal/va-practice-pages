The rule at the Kitab Society

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

The Kitab Society has met every Thursday for forty years in the back room of a Bakhar stationery shop. Its founding rule was absolute: no one speaks about the book who has not finished it. Members who arrived halfway through a novel sat silent with their tea, and the proud finishers held the floor.

Over the past year attendance dwindled from twenty to seven. The rule was still read aloud at the door, but everyone could see it being quietly bent: members whispered summaries to latecomers so they could pass as finishers, and the discussion thinned into polite recitals of plot. Nobody proposed changing the rule. It was the society's constitution, older than most of its members.

In March the new secretary, Rukmini, opened a meeting by admitting she had not finished the book. She spoke about the chapters she had read, asked questions about the rest, and to the older members' surprise the room came alive; even the silent half-readers joined in. Attendance has since climbed back to sixteen. Rukmini now argues that the finish-rule was killing the society and should be struck from the constitution.

Old Bishan, the longest-serving member, reads the same events differently. The problem was never the rule, he says, but the whispering around it: once members began pretending, finishing stopped meaning anything, and the discussion died of pretence, not of strictness. He points out that the meetings revived only after the whispering stopped, and that it is Rukmini's honesty, not her unfinished state, that people come for. Strike the rule, he warns, and the society becomes an ordinary chat group.

The rule remains on the door, unamended and unenforced. New members are told that it exists, and most finish the book anyway. Whatever else the season has settled, it has shown how rarely a society argues about what it is actually arguing about.
