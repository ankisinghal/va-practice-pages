The narrator who cannot be trusted

Test ID: Topic: Editorial difficulty: (a guess before reading, not a measured level)
Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes
Source: original passage written for practice, so full text is included.

A narrator who misleads the reader is among the most productive devices in fiction, precisely because it exploits an assumption we rarely examine: that the voice telling a story is, in the main, reliable. We accept a narrator's account of events as we accept a stranger's directions, provisionally and without audit. The unreliable narrator turns this habit against us.

The deception need not be deliberate. Many such narrators are sincere; they simply perceive events through vanity, grief or self-justification, and the reader gradually notices a gap between what is reported and what the evidence on the page suggests. A character who insists he bears no resentment while describing, in remarkable detail, every slight he has suffered has already contradicted himself. The irony lies in the distance between his claim and our understanding.

Critics sometimes treat this gap as a puzzle to be solved, as though the task were to recover "what really happened". That reading, though tempting, misses much of the pleasure. The point is seldom to catch the narrator out but to watch a mind construct its own version of events, and to recognise in that construction an exaggerated form of what everyone does when recounting their own life.

If so, the unreliable narrator is less an exception to ordinary storytelling than a magnifying glass held over it. We are invited not to distrust all narrators but to read with a more alert awareness of how any account is shaped by the one who gives it.
