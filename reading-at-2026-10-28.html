The ruler at the Vayu Club

Twenty-two years after she last played a competitive rally, Nandini returned to the Vayu Club's badminton hall, ready to be disappointed. In her memory the place had been severe: morning drills counted out loud, shuttles rationed one by one, and her coach, old Mr Verma, marking footwork with a wooden ruler. She expected sagging nets and a soft generation, and she had already composed the disappointed sentence she would offer the current coach.

What she found confused her script. The nets were new, but the floor was the same polished teak, with the same dead spot near the left service line, and a dozen children were doing something she did not recognise: rallying in silence with foam balls, then stopping to watch their own footwork on a tablet propped against the wall. It looked, she thought, like play. She asked the coach, a young woman named Farah, when the real training began.

Farah did not argue. She asked Nandini to hold the tablet for the next drill. The children repeated the same rally, and the screen slowed each step down: the same lunges Mr Verma had once marked with his ruler, now measured frame by frame. The foam balls, Farah said, forced the children to generate their own pace, and the silence was kept so they could hear their own feet. The old drills had been rationing and repetition; these were the same lessons wearing new clothes.

Nandini stayed for the whole session and never delivered her sentence. On the train home she admitted that she had not really returned to inspect the hall; she had returned to inspect her own youth, and finding it intact had required her to stop calling the present soft. The dead spot near the left service line still stole a point now and then, she noted with satisfaction, and some things, she decided, should never be repaired.
