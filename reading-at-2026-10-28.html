The lane book at Kesari

Words: Reading time: about 2 minutes

For three seasons, the Kesari district girls' sprint squad had won the morning heats and lost the evening finals. The coaches called it temperament, the local papers called it the finals curse, and every winter a new sports psychologist arrived with breathing charts. Some parents blamed the new synthetic track, though it had cost the district a year's budget. Meera, the squad's anchor runner, bore it quietly and kept running.

Ravi, the fifteen-year-old timekeeper's assistant, kept a lane book that nobody had asked for. He had started it to settle arguments about who ran which leg. He recorded not only times but gaps: how long each runner waited between her last warm-up and the starting gun. In the heats, which began at seven, Kesari's runners went from warm-up to blocks in under ten minutes. In the finals, held at six after a long afternoon of other events, the same runners waited forty minutes or more on cold benches behind the pavilion.

He showed the book to the new coach, Mrs D'Souza, expecting a polite nod. Instead she read it twice and asked the district committee for three small things: covered benches near the track, a second warm-up timed closer to the gun, and permission for runners to keep moving inside the call room. None of it involved courage training. At the next meet, Kesari's finalists ran almost exactly their heat times, and Meera won her first evening medal in two years.

The papers praised a mental breakthrough, which amused the squad. Nothing about their minds had changed; as far as anyone could tell, their legs had simply stopped going cold while they waited. Meera still keeps the breathing charts pinned above her locker, next to Ravi's lane book, and the runners joke that the charts cost a season while the notebook cost nothing.
